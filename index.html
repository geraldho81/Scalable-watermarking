Every word an AI writes was chosen out of thousands.

Some of those choices are a signature. This is how Google DeepMind hid one inside Gemini, why twenty million people did not notice, and how anyone holding the key can read it back without ever touching the model.

A reading of Scalable watermarking for identifying large language model outputs, Dathathri et al., Nature 634, 818-823, 23 October 2024. Roughly 15 minutes.

Start reading
01

It never writes a sentence. It writes a word.

It builds a ranked list, picks one, throws the list away, and starts again.

Ask a model to finish my favourite tropical fruit is and it does not reach for an answer. It builds a list.

Then it picks one off the list, sticks it on the end, throws the list away, and builds a brand new one from scratch for the next word. Every sentence you have ever read from an AI was assembled this way, one draw at a time.

Which means that for every word that made it onto the page, thousands were considered and thrown out. That discarding is the opening.

02

The obvious answers do not work.

Archives do not scale. Detectors accuse the wrong people.

One approach is to store every piece of text every model has ever produced, then check new writing against the archive. It works, in the sense that a phone book works. It also means retaining all of it, forever, with every privacy problem that follows, and coordinating that archive across every company running a model.

The other approach is a classifier that reads text and guesses. These go stale every time models improve, they are expensive to keep retraining, and they perform badly on writing they were not trained for. They also misfire hardest on people writing in a language that is not their first. Students have been accused of cheating on the strength of that output.

So the paper changes the question. Instead of trying to recognise AI text afterwards, can something be left behind while the text is being written?

03

Do not mark the text. Mark the choosing.

The mark goes into the choice, never into the sentence.

Here is the whole trick.

The watermark is not added to the finished sentence. Nothing is swapped for a synonym. No invisible characters are stitched between the words. Anything done to text after it is written leaves scars, and a careful reader or a determined attacker finds them.

Instead, the watermark changes how each word gets picked. The model still draws from its own list. It just runs a contest first, and the contest is quietly rigged by a secret only the model's owner holds.

The text that comes out is text the model would have been happy to write anyway. It is only across a few hundred words that its choices start leaning in a direction that looks like nothing at all, unless you know exactly what to check.

04

The same four words always roll the same dice.

Four words and a secret key decide every coin in the language.

Take the last four words written so far. Mix them with a secret key that never leaves the company. Out comes a number: the seed.

The seed is not random the way a thrown dice is random. It is repeatable. The same four words plus the same key produce the same seed on any machine, forever. That is the entire reason detection can work later, without the model and without asking anybody's permission.

The seed then hands every word in the model's vocabulary a score. In the paper's default setting that score is brutally simple: a zero or a one. A coin, flipped in advance, for every word in the language. mango might get a 1. lychee might get a 0. Change a single one of the four preceding words and every coin in the language flips again.

05

Eight words go in. One comes out.

A knockout bracket decides every word an AI writes.

When it is time to write the next word, the model does not draw once. It draws eight times from its own list, and puts the eight into a knockout bracket. Scroll to run the draw.

Round one. Four pairs. In each pair, the word holding the higher coin under the first set of dice wins. Ties are settled at random. Round two. The four survivors pair off again, judged by a second, independent set of dice. Round three. The last two, a third set. The survivor is written to the page. The other seven are thrown out, and there they stay.

Nothing was forced. All eight entrants were drawn from the model's own ranked list, so whichever one wins is something the model was already willing to say. But the winner also had to keep winning coin flips, and a word that wins three coin flips in a row is not the sort of accident you find in ordinary writing.

Do that four hundred times and you have four hundred small coincidences, all pointing the same way.

06

Twenty million answers. Nobody noticed.

A live experiment on real traffic, at the scale of a shipping product.

The obvious worry is that rigging the choice damages the writing.

The paper's answer is that the tournament never introduces a word the model would not have picked. All eight entrants came from its own distribution, so rigging which of them wins does not smuggle anything foreign onto the page.

Then they tested it in the open, which is the part that makes this a production paper rather than a proposal. A random slice of real Gemini traffic, then called Bard, was quietly served by the watermarked model. Roughly twenty million responses in, they compared the thumbs-up and thumbs-down that real people had left on both.

20,000,000real Gemini responses served by the watermarked model
+0.01%difference in thumbs-up rate, watermarked higher
−0.02%difference in thumbs-down rate, watermarked lower

Both differences fall well inside the 95% confidence interval.

They ran a controlled version too: 3,000 questions, watermarked and unwatermarked answers side by side, human raters scoring grammar and coherence, relevance, correctness, helpfulness and overall quality. No significant preference either way, on any of the five.

07

Detection is arithmetic.

The text and the key. Never the model.

To check a piece of text you need two things: the text, and the key. You do not need the model. You do not need to run anything expensive. You do not need to ask Google.

Walk the text word by word. At each word, take the four words before it, mix in the key, get the seed, and look up which coin that word was handed. Write down the zero or the one. Do that for every word and every set of dice, then take the average.

Text nobody watermarked averages about 0.5, because nobody was steering the coins. Watermarked text averages higher, because every word on the page won its way there by winning coin flips. Then you draw a line and say: above this, watermarked.

08

A watermark needs room to hide.

Length and freedom decide whether the mark can be read at all.

Length. Every word adds one more small piece of evidence, and none of them is worth much alone. Forty words is close to a coin flip. Four hundred words is close to certain.

Freedom. This one is the real limit. Ask what is the capital of France and there is one answer. The model's list has a single word on it with any weight at all. The tournament has nothing to choose between, so there is nothing to encode, and no watermark survives.

Ask for a story, an explanation, an argument, and the list is wide open. Hundreds of reasonable next words, and every choice among them is somewhere to leave a mark. The paper's word for this is entropy.

09

It had to be free, or it would never run.

Thirty rounds deep, and it costs 0.088 milliseconds a word.

A watermark that slows the model down does not get deployed, whatever its merits. Much of this paper is really an engineering paper about that.

In production the tournament runs thirty rounds deep, not three. More rounds means more coin flips per word, which means more evidence and a steadier score. The benefit tails off with depth, so thirty is a chosen stopping point rather than a limit.

They also fitted it to speculative sampling, the trick where a small fast model guesses several words ahead and the big model checks its work. That combination is how large systems actually run, and nobody had made watermarking work with it before. SynthID-Text runs in Gemini and Gemini Advanced today, and the code is public.

10

This is not a solved problem.

Paraphrase it and the signature is gone.

The paper is unusually direct about its own limits, and any honest reading has to carry them. They decide what the method is actually for.

  1. Paraphrasing removes it

    Run watermarked text through another model and ask for a rewrite, and the mark mostly goes. Heavy editing by hand does the same. Light edits survive, because the score is an average and a few changed words barely move it.

  2. It only works if the writer cooperates

    The watermark has to be applied at the moment of generation, by whoever is running the model. Anyone running an open-weight model on their own hardware simply does not switch it on. The paper names this directly as a problem that gets harder as open models spread.

  3. It can be attacked

    Stealing, spoofing and scrubbing are live research problems. Someone able to probe the system enough might learn the pattern well enough to strip it out, or worse, to stamp it onto text a human actually wrote.

  4. Short text carries nothing

    A sentence has almost no evidence in it. A tweet is out of reach. So is a headline, a caption, or a factual answer with only one sensible phrasing.

  5. It is not an AI detector

    It answers did this particular system write this. It does not answer was this written by AI. Those are different questions, and the second one still has no good answer.

The authors' own framing: generative watermarking is complementary to the other approaches rather than a replacement for them, and no text detection method is foolproof. What they have shown is that one of these methods can run at the scale of a real product without making it worse.

Your sentence. Your discards.

Your key, your sentence, and everything the tournament threw away.

12

Now take it out of yours.

The paper names the one thing that defeats a generative watermark. You already watched it work.

Everything above this line is a reading of somebody else's research. This last part is mine.

In section 07 you moved a real score. Watermarked text sat at 0.607. The same text, paraphrased, fell to 0.521, close enough to chance that no threshold worth using would flag it. You ran that arithmetic yourself, on this page, a few thousand words ago.

It works because of the mechanism this whole page has been about. The mark is a record of which words got chosen, held in the choices themselves rather than stitched into the sentence. Replace the words and there is no longer a record. Which means the only thing that removes a generative watermark is the one thing nobody wants to hear: actually rewriting the prose.

Before any of that, though, there is a much dumber problem sitting in your text right now.

Two different things are called a watermark

Only one of them is what this paper describes, and people conflate them constantly.

What that box cannot do

It cannot see a statistical watermark, and neither can anything else you run locally. There is no hidden character to find, no metadata to strip, nothing sitting in the file. The signal is spread across hundreds of word choices under a key you do not have. Any tool that tells you it detected and removed one is lying to you.

Moving that signal means replacing the words, at the token level, across the whole piece. And there is a trap in doing it: a rewrite performed by a model that watermarks its own output just swaps one company's mark for another's.

Which is what I built Slopstopper for

It does both layers and keeps them separate, because they are different jobs. Layer A strips every character in the table above. Layer B is opt-in, and it is a real rewrite: token-level paraphrase, changed clause order, changed sentence boundaries, run through a model chosen because it does not mark its own output.

On the way through it also removes the things that make writing read as machine-written to a human rather than to a detector: the "it's not X, it's Y" tic, em dashes, throat-clearing openers, fake-profound closers, the rule of three. Your facts, numbers and voice stay.

It will not make your text undetectable, and it does not claim to. Short text carries almost no statistical signal either way; long text carries more, and a rewrite reduces it rather than guaranteeing its removal. That limit is written into the tool's own documentation, and any project promising otherwise is selling something.
